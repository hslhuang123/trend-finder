Help

TrendFinder lets you practise investing with pretend money. It checks a list of stocks, ranks them, lets you "buy" and "sell" them, and shows how you would have done. Prices arrive late, and none of this is real advice.

The four tabs

  • Portfolio — your pretend account: your cash, what you own, and whether you're up or down.
  • Screener — ranks the stocks you're watching and shows which ones look strongest right now.
  • Backtest — tests your plan against old prices to see if it would have made or lost money.
  • Help — this page.

Portfolio

  • The big numbers at the top show your total value, your cash, what your stocks are worth, and your profit or loss. Everything is in US dollars.
  • Performance is a line showing how your account changed over time, plus a few scores. It shows up after a couple of days, once there's enough history to draw.
  • Under it, each stock you own shows what you paid, what it's worth now, and whether you're up or down.
  • Signals are quick hints next to each holding: HOLD keep it, REVIEW maybe sell some, SELL time to get out. Hover over the hint to see why (for example, it dropped past your stop-loss).
  • Canadian stocks are shown in Canadian dollars, but the app converts them to US dollars so your totals add up.

Screener

  • Momentum is a score for how strongly the price has been rising lately. Higher means it's been climbing more.
  • AI is the robot's opinion. Hover over the badge to see how strong the trend is, how risky it is, how likely it is to be a good buy, and its overall call — buy now, watch, or avoid. It also gives a combined "Final score" that decides the order of the list.
  • ★ and bold mark the app's own top picks from your list.
  • 🔍 marks a couple of extra stocks the app found elsewhere in the market just for that run. They aren't saved to your list, and they change each time.
  • Hover over any column title to see what it means.

How to read a Backtest

A backtest answers one question: if you had followed your plan in the past, would you have made money or lost money?

Your plan is just the set of rules you choose at the top of this page: how many stocks to hold, how often to swap them, and when to sell. The backtest looks at old prices and follows those rules.

What the settings mean

Years
How far back to test. Longer covers more market conditions.
Top K
How many stocks to hold at the same time. Each one gets an equal share of your money. This only caps how many you hold at once — the plan still buys and sells many different stocks over the years.
Rebalance (days)
How often the app re-ranks the list and swaps what you hold, counted in trading days. 5 is about once a week.
Stop-loss %
Sell if the price falls this far below what you paid. A safety net that limits losses.
Take-profit %
Sell once the price rises this far above what you paid.
Trailing %
Sell if the price falls this far from its highest point since you bought. Leave blank to turn it off.
Commission bps
The fee charged on each trade, in basis points (1 bp = 0.01%; 5 bps = 0.05%).
Slippage bps
A small allowance for getting a slightly worse price than you expected, in basis points.

What is SPY?

SPY is a fund you can buy that tracks the S&P 500 — a basket of the 500 biggest US companies. It is used here to stand in for "the whole US market". If a plan can't beat simply buying SPY and doing nothing, it isn't adding much.

Was it a winner or a loser?

  • The line — the blue line is your plan, and the grey dashed line is what would have happened if you had bought the whole US market (SPY) and done nothing. If the blue line ends higher than it started, you made money. If it ends above the grey line, you did better than just leaving your money alone.
  • The score cards — a plus in "Total return" or "CAGR" means you made money; a minus means you lost. A Sharpe above 0 is good. Max drawdown shows the worst dip along the way.
  • The trades table — a minus in the P&L column means that single trade lost money.

The answer depends on the years you pick

There is no single answer to "did it win?". It changes with the time period, and with whether you compare against simply doing nothing. Here's one example:

One more thing: a plan like this often loses more trades than it wins, yet still ends up ahead — because the wins are bigger than the losses. So look at the line and the score cards, not just how many trades won.

Why the trades table is so long

Each row is one single buy or sell — not a different company. Only the latest 200 rows are shown; the real total is on the "Trades" card. Your plan trades often, so the same few companies appear again and again. The "Reason" column says why each trade happened: it was a new pick, it dropped off the list, it hit a stop-loss, it hit a take-profit, or it hit a trailing stop.

The same settings can give different numbers. A backtest gives the same answer for the same old prices. But this app fetches prices live, and they can be refreshed or revised — so two runs can differ. Treat any single run as a rough guide, not a precise result.

What is Jev?

Jev is a robot that makes decisions instead of writing sentences. You show it some facts and ask simple questions, and it answers with a choice plus how sure it is. The app uses it in three ways:

  • When picking stocks — how strong the trend is, how risky it is, and whether to buy.
  • When you already own a stock — whether to keep it, sell a bit, or sell all.
  • For your whole account — how risky it is overall and whether it's balanced.

Jev only gives advice. It never buys or sells for you, and its advice isn't tested in the backtest.

What the scores mean

Final value
How much your pretend account is worth at the end.
Total return
How much you made or lost in total, as a percentage.
CAGR
The average growth per year.
Volatility
How bumpy the ride was.
Sharpe
How much you earned for the risk you took. Around 1 or more is good; below 0 means you lost money.
Sortino
Like Sharpe, but it only counts the bad bumps.
Max drawdown
The biggest drop from a high point — how bad the worst dip got.
Win rate
How often your trades made money.

Things to know

  • Prices arrive late and come from one free source. They can be missing or wrong.
  • The backtest assumes you trade at the day's closing price and pay small fees. It ignores things a real broker handles, like taxes.
  • The app only looks at a small number of stocks, so results can be misleading.
  • This is for learning. It is not financial advice, and past results don't predict the future.
